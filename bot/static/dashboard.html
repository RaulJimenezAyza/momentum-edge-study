Pretend money. This robot places no real orders and holds no real coins. It reads live prices and simulates what would have happened.

What am I looking at?

A trading robot watching 50 crypto coins, one price update a minute. When a coin drops much faster than that particular coin normally moves, and unusually many people are trading it, the robot buys — betting the fall went too far and the price will bounce back.

Every buy and every sell pays a fee to the exchange. Measured over months of real prices, the bounce is too small to pay for the fees — the fees are about four times bigger. So this loses money on purpose-built, carefully tested rules. That is the finding, not a bug.

And the bounce itself may not be real. It came out positive in every month tested, always by about the same tiny amount. But the entry rule fires on twenty coins at once whenever the whole market dips, so those twenty results are really closer to one result. Counting them properly, the effect is no longer distinguishable from luck. Consistent, small, and unproven — three separate things, all true at the same time.

The chart shows it directly: the blue line is the account, the orange line is what it would be worth if trading were free. The gap between them is the fee bill.

The big table at the bottom lists all 50 coins and, for each one, why the robot is not buying it right now. Almost always the answer is "nothing unusual is happening" — which is what should happen. Hover any column heading to see what it means.
